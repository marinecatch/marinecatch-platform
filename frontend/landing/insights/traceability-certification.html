From Catch to Certification: How Digital Traceability Is Reshaping Eco-Labelling for African Fishers

July 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  MarineCatch Africa

Kenya's coastal fishers land some of the Indian Ocean's finest seafood. Yet much of this catch never reaches premium markets, not because of quality, but because of documentation.

The Certification Gap

The European Union, the world's largest seafood import market, requires that every fish entering its borders be accompanied by a catch certificate, a document proving the fish was legally caught, properly handled, and traceable to a verified vessel and landing site.

Most small-scale fishers cannot produce this document. Not because they fish illegally, but because the digital infrastructure to record, verify and transmit catch data has never existed at the landing site level.

The result: premium markets remain out of reach for the fishers who need them most, while large processors with their own compliance infrastructure capture the export margin.

What Digital Traceability Actually Means

Traceability is not a compliance form. It is a chain of verified events — from the moment fish leaves the water to the moment it reaches a buyer's facility. When every link in that chain is digitally recorded, the result is a catch certificate that satisfies regulatory requirements without the administrative burden that excludes small-scale producers.

On the MarineCatch platform, when a fisher logs a catch — whether via USSD from a basic phone or through a smartphone — the system automatically records:

  • Fisher identity and BMU membership verification
  • Vessel registration and gear type
  • Species, weight and landing site with timestamp
  • Cold chain custody through storage and delivery
  • Quality inspection results at each handover point

By the time that catch reaches a hotel kitchen or a processor's facility, a complete digital provenance record exists — exportable as a catch certificate, readable by import compliance systems, and verifiable by any buyer in the chain.

The Traceability Chain in Practice

A single verified transaction on the MarineCatch platform generates: fisher identity record → BMU verification → catch event → cold chain log → quality inspection → buyer receipt → digital catch certificate. Every step is timestamped, tamper-evident, and exportable.

Stepwise Certification Pathways

Full MSC certification typically takes 18–24 months and requires formal fisheries assessment — a process most small-scale fishers cannot access individually. MarineCatch supports an incremental pathway that builds toward certification using data already captured operationally:

1
Community-Verified Sustainable Catch BMU records + digital catch logging + IUU compliance verification
2
Fisheries Improvement Project (FIP) Compliance Stock data integration + gear type recording + sustainability scoring
3
Full Eco-Label Certification MSC, Friend of the Sea, or Eco-Mark Africa — supported by operational data already in the system

Each stage builds on data already captured through normal platform operations. No separate compliance system is needed — the certification evidence is a byproduct of running the business.

Group Certification Through Fisher Clusters

Individual certification is expensive. MarineCatch's cluster model pools small-scale fishers into verified digital groups for joint certification — lowering audit costs and improving market access for fishers who could never afford certification individually.

A cluster of 15 fishers sharing one certification cost is commercially viable. Fifteen individual certifications are not. The cluster model doesn't just make certification affordable — it creates the collective supply volume that makes export markets worth pursuing.

Alignment with African Standards

MarineCatch aligns traceability data with Eco-Mark Africa and SASSI green-list standards — offering a homegrown eco-labelling pathway that reduces dependence on foreign audit bodies whose criteria often disadvantage small-scale African producers.

Northern eco-label standards were largely designed for industrial fisheries. Small-scale fishers — who represent the majority of Africa's seafood production and provide critical food security and livelihoods for coastal communities, are frequently excluded or disadvantaged by criteria that assume industrial-scale documentation and audit infrastructure.

MarineCatch's data infrastructure provides the evidence base to advocate for standards that recognize the full contribution of small-scale fisheries, working with NGOs, regional bodies, and the African Union's Blue Economy Strategy.

The Strategic Opportunity

For Fishers Digital traceability opens eco-label access, delivering 15–40% price premiums in export markets and recognition of sustainable practices.
For Buyers Transparent sourcing with full provenance reduces reputational and compliance risk, enabling credible sustainability claims.
For Governments Catch volume data and stock health indicators provide evidence for inclusive fisheries policy and stronger global trade positioning.
For Africa's Seafood Sector Regional eco-labels reduce dependence on Northern standards, positioning African fisheries on African terms.

Building the Foundation Now

Certification is not an overnight process. But the groundwork, digital catch recording, chain of custody documentation, cluster verification, quality inspection records, can be built now, and is being built now, through every transaction on the MarineCatch platform.

Every lot logged via USSD, every delivery confirmed, every quality inspection recorded is data that builds the compliance case. The fish is already there. The markets are ready. The infrastructure is what has been missing.
